Dynamic Allocation under Economic Shocks

01ABSTRACT

Abstract

This paper studies whether contingent restrictions on the use and conversion of public transfer claims can improve insurance against heterogeneous economic shocks. Rather than presuming that earmarked liquidity is intrinsically superior to fungible money, it treats segmentation as a costly institutional technology whose value depends on externalities, incomplete insurance, supplier capacity, private information, and fiscal settlement. An economy with heterogeneous households, irreversible capability losses, domain-specific providers, a network of legally admissible conversions, and aggregate and idiosyncratic shocks is developed. The analysis establishes an equivalence result for inframarginal restrictions; a welfare condition for binding restrictions; an existence and uniqueness result for a regularized routing problem; an accounting proposition showing why end-user restrictions do not by themselves extinguish fiscal obligations; a stability condition for adaptive feedback; and a constrained-planner characterization with downside-risk penalties. The paper also implements a fully labeled synthetic computational experiment involving four transfer regimes under the same authorized budget. In the benchmark parameterization, adaptive restricted assistance improves average simulated welfare relative to unrestricted transfers, but the ranking reverses under scarce supply. The quantitative exercise is a mechanism test, not a causal evaluation of a real-world policy. Published randomized and administrative evidence is reviewed as external motivation, without treating it as validation of the proposed architecture. The resulting theory identifies a limited but potentially valuable role for purpose-bound liquidity: preventing high-social-cost interruptions when a verifiable critical-use margin is binding, administrative errors are controllable, and provider capacity and fiscal settlement can accommodate the intervention.
