Empirical Foundations of a Purpose-Bound Economy

Evidence on Household Demand, Program Scale, Market Adjustment, and Long-Run Development

01ABSTRACT

Abstract

This paper develops a disciplined empirical foundation for a broader research program in purposebound finance, universal meal rights, human-capital access, supplier contracting, market competition, and international welfare coordination. The proposed purpose-bound monetary architecture has not been implemented at the proposed global scale. Consequently, this paper neither estimates the causal effect of that architecture nor represents simulations as observed evidence. Instead, it separates the mechanisms on which the architecture relies and studies them with five independently verifiable bodies of observed data, combined with a narrowly interpreted review of existing randomized evidence. The original empirical exercises comprise: food expenditure and income for 235 historical Belgian households; an international panel of 142 countries observed at five-year intervals between 1952 and 2007; 203 quarters of United States macroeconomic data from 1959Q1 through 2009Q3; United States Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program national administrative totals for fiscal years 2000–2025; and a selected 24country cross-section of OECD public social expenditure for 2024. The observational estimates are computed from archived real datasets with heteroskedasticity-robust, country-clustered, or heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation-consistent uncertainty estimates as appropriate. Household food expenditure is increasing but less than proportional to income; however, the historical Engel sample contains no transfer experiment. The conditional income–longevity association remains positive in a two-way fixed-effects panel but changes substantially under population weighting and regional restrictions. Investment growth in historical US data is several times more volatile than consumption growth, but contemporaneous output–investment regressions do not identify a demand-induced investment effect. The SNAP administrative series documents a large, sustained, financed restricted-transfer program but does not identify its general-equilibrium supply consequences. A separate review of published experiments distinguishes directly established treatment effects from analogy. The results support a modular empirical research agenda, not a verdict that a global purpose-bound monetary system is welfare-improving. The paper specifies a source-to-claim audit, formal identification boundaries, sensitivity tests, equilibrium accounting restrictions, and a falsifiable design for future multi-market trials. A separate analytical extension derives conditional thresholds for price adjustment, credible supplier investment, and incremental welfare superiority relative to equal-cost cash support with identical capacity procurement. Its numerical cases are disclosed hypothetical scenarios, not estimated treatment effects.
