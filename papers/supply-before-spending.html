Supply Before Spending

Industrial Capacity, Entrepreneurial Entry, and Demand Commitments in a Purpose-Bound Economy

DL
Donghyun LeeIndependent Researcher
DL / RESEARCH2026
D/L.
Innovation & Industry37
01ABSTRACT

Abstract

This paper develops a theory of supply-first demand commitments: an institutional arrangement in which future purchasing power is credibly specified before it is spent, private productive capacity is built and verified before large-scale redemption occurs, and market access is subsequently activated in accordance with measured supply conditions. The mechanism is designed for a purpose-bound monetary system (PoC), in which transfers are restricted to specified goods or services, subject to eligibility, expiry, and audit rules, and ordinarily cannot be converted into unrestricted currency. The central innovation is not the proposition that productive capacity is desirable before expenditure. It is the design of an intertemporal coordination mechanism that joins credible demand commitments, endogenous scale investment by incumbent firms, venture entry and experimentation, complementarity across upstream and downstream industries, capacity-contingent transfer activation, and a consolidated public balance-sheet constraint. A dynamic heterogeneous-firm model nests fixed costs, endogenous product variety, financial frictions, quality investment, input-output dependencies, imperfect information about future demand, and an occasionally binding capacity constraint. We characterize the investment and entry margins, conditions under which commitments can select a higher-capacity equilibrium, and circumstances in which delayed activation reduces the price response to transfers. The principal propositions are conditional: investment requires commitment credibility; rivalry can erode inefficient markups only if contracting avoids foreclosure; supply investment itself can produce transitional inflation; and an erroneous forecast or an overly generous guarantee may generate overcapacity, fiscal losses, and concentration. A complementary empirical program specifies contract-level data construction, staggered-adoption difference-in-differences, event-study identification, regression discontinuity, randomized phase-in, structural estimation, and transparent counterfactual simulation. Existing evidence on advance market commitments and industrial policy motivates the framework but does not constitute causal proof that a PoC system would outperform unrestricted transfers. The paper concludes with an implementable policy architecture and explicitly testable rejection criteria.
