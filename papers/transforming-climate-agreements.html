Transforming Climate Agreements

Innovation, Market Guarantees, and Economies of Scale

DL

01ABSTRACT

Abstract

The central impediment to global environmental innovation is not merely the absence of a sufficiently high price on pollution. It is the absence, in a number of technologically demanding sectors, of a credible market against which an innovator can finance investment in technologies specifically suited to lower-income industrializing economies. This paper proposes a treaty-based market-making institution in which participating governments commit ex ante to procure and deploy eligible environmental technologies at preannounced quantities, prices, and dates, subject to technical validation and financial closure. Inventors that deliver superior solutions may receive substantial, potentially exclusive, commercial territories or contracted market tranches. Concentration is thus treated as an endogenous instrument of innovation policy rather than an outcome to be suppressed independently of the underlying economics. The institution combines competition for future markets, conditional purchase obligations, scale-sensitive price schedules, risk-sharing finance, and contestable renewal. A set of models characterizes research incentives, portfolio allocation, production economies, technology compatibility, supplier failure, intertemporal welfare, and sovereign participation. A descriptive empirical analysis combines International Renewable Energy Agency technology-level cost and capacity series with evidence on pneumococcal advance market commitments, the Montreal Protocol, and cross-country financing constraints. The data exhibit substantial but heterogeneous cost reductions; the results do not identify a causal effect of market concentration or establish the superiority of the proposed treaty. A transparent illustrative numerical model nevertheless shows why a dominant supplier can be preferable to either symmetric fragmentation or complete exclusivity, and why the ranking reverses as financing credibility, local fit, and supply disruption change. The appropriate policy comparison is between complete feasible institutional packages, not between an ideal competitive market and a legally protected monopoly in isolation. The proposed architecture makes verified deployment an enforceable commercial transaction while retaining the environmental target as a performance test, not as a substitute for the market commitment.
