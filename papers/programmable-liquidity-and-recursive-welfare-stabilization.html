Toward a Computational Theory of Monetary Coordination

01ABSTRACT

Abstract

This paper develops a theoretical framework for purpose-bound monetary systems in which liquidity is no longer treated as fully fungible, but instead operates through constitutionally differentiated and recursively programmable domains of economic coordination. The central argument of the paper is that persistent inequality and recursive welfare instability emerge not solely from ex post distributive failures, but from the structural dynamics of unrestricted fungible liquidity itself. Under conventional monetary architectures, capability-preserving expenditures such as healthcare, education, nutrition, and long-term developmental investment remain continuously vulnerable to speculative displacement, congestion amplification, and recursive concentration dynamics. As a consequence, economic inequality evolves not merely as a static distributional phenomenon, but as a self-reinforcing stochastic process of capability divergence. To address this problem, the paper introduces a recursive segmented monetary framework in which liquidity possesses domain-specific admissibility conditions, adaptive routing constraints, and constitutionally bounded convertibility structures. The proposed system integrates recursive mechanism design, stochastic control theory, segmented general equilibrium analysis, network congestion dynamics, capability-based welfare theory, and programmable CBDC architecture into a unified institutional model. The economy is represented as a high-dimensional stochastic coordination system in which capability accumulation, liquidity propagation, trust evolution, congestion spillovers, and recursive policy intervention interact continuously over time. The paper derives conditions under which Recursive Segmented General Equilibrium exists and demonstrates that recursive capability dynamics remain bounded under appropriate congestion-control and routing constraints. Spectral stability analysis establishes conditions for stable liquidity propagation across segmented monetary networks, while mean-field convergence results show that large heterogeneous-agent economies admit tractable distributional approximation. Computational simulations further suggest that segmented liquidity systems may reduce recursive inequality amplification, improve welfare resilience under large stochastic shocks, stabilize capability persistence, and generate lower long-run congestion volatility relative to unrestricted fungible monetary systems. Beyond its macroeconomic implications, the paper additionally develops a constitutional theory of programmable monetary governance. Because recursively routable liquidity systems possess unprecedented informational and institutional capacity, the framework incorporates formal constraints governing observability, convertibility rights, emergency intervention, judicial review, and decentralized verification. The paper further analyzes the potential role of artificial intelligence and reinforcement learning in adaptive monetary coordination, while emphasizing the necessity of constitutionally bounded AI governance within recursive policylearning environments. More broadly, the paper argues that future monetary systems may increasingly evolve from passive exchange infrastructures into adaptive coordination architectures designed to stabilize long-run capability continuity rather than merely facilitate unrestricted transactional fungibility. The framework therefore proposes a shift in monetary theory away from homogeneous liquidity equilibrium models and toward recursively governed systems of programmable institutional coordination.
