Designing Money

Purpose-Bound Currency and the End of Macroeconomic Constraints

DL
Donghyun LeeIndependent Researcher
DL / RESEARCH2026
D/L.
Monetary Architecture06
01ABSTRACT

Abstract

This paper develops a new monetary framework in which allocation is embedded directly into the structure of money. In standard macroeconomic models, money is assumed to be fungible, and policy operates by adjusting aggregate conditions through interest rates, fiscal transfers, or regulation. This architecture generates well-known constraints, including cross-sector spillovers, financial amplification, and the limits of monetary autonomy in open economies. We propose an alternative system based on purpose-bound currency (PoC), a class of nonfungible monetary instruments restricted to specific domains of use. By construction, PoC eliminates cross-domain substitutability and embeds allocation at the point of issuance. We show that this seemingly simple modification has far-reaching implications for equilibrium structure. First, in a general equilibrium framework, PoC induces segmentation: consumption, prices, and liquidity become domain-specific, and cross-sector spillovers are structurally eliminated. Second, in both discrete-time DSGE and continuous-time stochastic control models, we demonstrate that financial amplification mechanisms and global financial cycle transmission do not propagate into PoC-governed domains. Third, in a mechanism design setting, we show that incentive compatibility is achieved by construction, as misallocation becomes infeasible rather than merely suboptimal. These results imply that several canonical constraints in macroeconomics—including aspects of the monetary trilemma—are not fundamental, but arise from the use of a single, fungible monetary instrument. When monetary functions are separated across domain-specific currencies, these constraints are relaxed at the structural level. The paper provides a unified theoretical framework—combining general equilibrium, dynamic macroeconomics, and mechanism design—to show that monetary architecture itself can serve as a policy instrument.
