Firms, Networks, and Collective Innovation

01ABSTRACT

Abstract

This paper develops a general theory of the National Industrial Federation (NIF): an institutional architecture that preserves private ownership, firm-level managerial autonomy, entry, exit, and product-market competition while coordinating selected complementary resources at the level of an industrial ecosystem. The motivating problem is not that all decentralization is inefficient. Rival experimentation and dispersed information are fundamental sources of discovery. Rather, when research facilities are indivisible, knowledge spillovers cannot be fully appropriated, specialized workers face search frictions, and supplychain investments exhibit complementarities, the individually rational organization of firms can leave socially valuable connections unrealized. Conversely, centralized industrial planning concentrates informational and political risk. The NIF is proposed as a third organizational arrangement: decentralized decisions connected through contestable, transparent, and limitedpurpose common institutions. Its main instruments are voluntary or rule-governed access to shared research infrastructure, interoperable training and credentials, industry-wide labor matching, jointly financed precompetitive innovation, publicly auditable innovation portfolios, and safeguards for market contestability. The paper builds a multi-layer general-equilibrium framework with heterogeneous firms and workers, network spillovers, endogenous participation, congestible collective facilities, public finance, and innovation under uncertainty. It derives conditions under which federation is welfare improving, shows why excessive concentration or an overbroad federation can reverse the result, and proposes empirically falsifiable predictions. A detailed research protocol combines staggered-adoption causal inference, network exposure mappings, quasi-experiments, randomized access lotteries, firmworker linked panels, patent citations, and structural counterfactuals. Cross-country institutions are discussed as partial comparators, not as evidence that the proposed comprehensive model already exists. No original causal estimate is claimed; the quantitative illustrations are hypothetical and are explicitly separated from observed evidence. The paper argues that the appropriate unit of industrial policy may sometimes be the network of complementary firms, rather than the subsidized firm or the state-owned champion, subject to strict competition, fiscal, and democratic-accountability constraints.
