The Economics of Preference Diversity

Democracy, Coordination, and Collective Choice

DL

Abstract

This paper develops a political-economic framework for democratic choice under irreducible preference diversity, combining combinatorial social-choice analysis with a strategically founded model of consultation, lobbying, information transmission, agenda control, and electoral accountability. It complements, rather than disputes, Arrow's impossibility theorem. Strict linear orders over n alternatives number n!, while directed circular arrangements modulo rotation number (n−1)!, independent of electorate size; neither count implies that actual pairwise majorities must cycle. Our normative claim is that pluralist democracy need not seek a unique complete public preference ordering as the output of every choice procedure. Its institutional task is to reconcile feasible concurrent policies and govern unavoidable conflicts through legitimate, contestable procedures. We propose regular election of representatives, transparent legislative votes, and recurring, primarily advisory online policy consultation with fiscal and constitutional safeguards. The central theoretical contribution endogenizes participation distortion previously treated as a fixed bias: citizens choose whether to participate, organized groups choose costly mobilization, an informed expert sends strategic cheap-talk messages, and a reelection-sensitive politician chooses agenda access and policy. In a tractable sequential game we characterize a perfect Bayesian equilibrium, establish uniqueness of organized mobilization under stated conditions, derive informative two-message communication cutoffs, and obtain an explicit agenda-inclusion threshold. The equilibrium online-public opinion gap arises from the interaction of mobilization technology and electoral incentives; population calibration removes this specific incentive under stringent observability and exclusion assumptions. Analytical counterfactuals, exact preference-profile enumeration, synthetic THE ECONOMICS OF PREFERENCE DIVERSITY • RESEARCH MANUSCRIPT simulations, and an external literature review clarify when additional consultation may improve information and when it may strengthen strategic capture. Empirical identification protocols are provided, but no causal effect of the full proposal is asserted without data. The contribution is an analytically disciplined framework for democratic coordination when disagreement persists and political agents behave strategically.
