Beyond Universal Basic Income: Can Purpose-Bound Transfers Deliver More Welfare with Less Inflation?

Monetary Financing, Endogenous Supply, and Social Welfare

DL
Donghyun LeeIndependent Researcher
DL / RESEARCH2026
D/L.
Welfare & Capabilities04
01ABSTRACT

Abstract

Universal basic income (UBI) and purpose-bound money (PoC) are often compared as alternative instruments for redistributing purchasing power. That comparison is incomplete without examining financing, settlement, behavioral responses, endogenous supply, and the consolidated balance sheet of the state. This paper develops a fiscal–monetary framework in which unconditional transfers and purpose-restricted transfers can be financed either by taxes, public debt, or money creation; recipients optimize across eligible and ineligible goods; suppliers endogenously adjust capacity, quality, and prices; and central-bank liabilities change composition as restricted claims are redeemed. The central analytical result is conditional rather than categorical. Restricted transfers may generate more targeted consumption and lower sectoral inflation than unrestricted cash when procurement, competition, and elastic supply jointly offset the additional demand concentration. Without credible supply expansion, however, earmarking can intensify rather than alleviate sector-specific inflation. Furthermore, denomination in noninterest-bearing digital claims does not by itself avoid interest-bearing reserve liabilities after redemption; expiration reduces only outstanding claims that have genuinely lapsed, while taxoffset redemption may substitute foregone public revenue for monetary liabilities. We examine these propositions alongside randomized evidence from cash and voucher programs in Ecuador, Mexico, Kenya, Malawi, and the United States, and construct an independent descriptive series from U.S. SNAP fiscal data for 1990–2025. A transparent structural sensitivity exercise exhibits an explicit supply-response threshold at which PoC’s target-sector price effect becomes smaller than that of cash, together with conditional reserve-remuneration trajectories. The evidence Beyond Universal Basic Income · 1 establishes neither unconditional superiority of PoC nor the equivalence of restricted digital claims to conventional in-kind transfers. Instead, it identifies testable design conditions under which the coupling of purchasing power with verifiable supply commitments may improve welfare per unit of consolidated fiscal–monetary cost.
