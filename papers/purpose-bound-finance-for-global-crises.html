Purpose-Bound Finance for Global Crises

Direct Supranational Intervention, Sovereign Participation, and State-Contingent Monetary Architecture

DL
Donghyun LeeIndependent Researcher

D/L.

01ABSTRACT

Abstract

This paper develops a theory of purpose-bound finance for risks that cross borders, propagate through production networks, and overwhelm nationally segmented fiscal mechanisms. Its institutional object is a proposed Global Central Bank (GCB), ordinarily responsible for a shared issuance, verification, and settlement protocol within which sovereign governments retain policy discretion. During a qualifying international emergency, however, the same institution may activate a treaty-limited direct intervention mode: it becomes the contracting principal for contingent service capacity, the issuer of separately funded emergency purchasing entitlements, the allocator of cross-border procurement, and the accountable obligor for certified supplier claims. The transition is not a suspension of scarcity, national law, or fiscal accountability. We distinguish nonconvertible beneficiary rights from merchant receivables and the liquid resources needed to discharge them. A general dynamic model combines heterogeneous sovereigns, endogenous supplier entry and preparedness, private information, correlated catastrophes, monetary and real-resource constraints, and an imperfect crisis signal. Three applications develop distinct mechanisms: a multicountry epidemic with infection externalities and treatment capacity; natural disasters that simultaneously destroy production and transport networks; and persistent climate risk with international mitigation spillovers and locally appropriable adaptation. Conditional propositions characterize the optimal emergency trigger, benefits of pooled reserves, supply-first investment thresholds, sovereign participation, and robust settlement constraints. Artificial intelligence enters as a costly information technology whose predictive and assignment value must be evaluated against conventional rules, strategic data manipulation, and safety constraints. Synthetic calculations illustrate both welfare improvements and reversals as correlation, institutional delay, and fiscal costs vary. The framework does not claim that programmable balances create real resources or that a new global institution is invariably superior to existing contingency finance, competitive procurement, or ordinary vouchers. Its contribution is to characterize when treaty-bounded supranational intervention can coordinate verifiable real services more effectively than decentralized arrangements at an equal full economic cost.
